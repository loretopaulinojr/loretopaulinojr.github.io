## Graduate School Applications: You Reap What You Sow


7 college applications. 4 Rejections. 3 Offers. 1 Interview.
1 fellowship application. 1 offer. 

There are many ways to begin this blog, but to be organized, let’s put things in chronological order. On December 31, 2024, I submitted my last application, to the University of Rhode Island Graduate School of Oceanography, for their Biological Oceanography program. This was the third Biological Oceanography program I applied to, along with Woods Hole Oceanographic Institution and the University of California, San Diego (Scripps Institution of Oceanography). The remaining schools I applied to were the University of Hawaii at Manoa, Oregon State University, Yale, and Boston University.

After I submitted all my applications, I remember that the number one school I wanted to attend was Yale. I applied to their Ecology and Evolutionary Biology program. I was really excited about Yale because I had a great meeting with a potential mentor whose research interests aligned with mine, and she was incredibly easy to talk to! As a research fellow at Boston University, I’d heard nothing but positive things about her from colleagues who had worked with her. Between that one meeting and the praise from her colleagues, it was hard not to imagine a future at Yale.
My only concern? The location. New Haven. I tried to convince myself it would be fine, it’s near both New York City and Boston, right? Yeah... that fantasy got shut down real quick by a former mentor who said, “No one moves to New Haven to go to New York or Boston.” Despite all the skepticism from people who knew me well, I overlooked the location for the potential mentorship and research opportunities.

The first official decision I received came on January 30, 2025, from the University of Rhode Island Graduate School of Oceanography: Accepted. Funny how the last application I submitted was the first one I heard back from. Thank you for that, Rhode Island. I remember meeting with the professor I listed on my application that same month, and I loved that meeting. She was the first person to ask me what it was like to be a trained chemist in a predominantly biology-focused lab. Looking back, I now realize it was her way of asking how I planned to address my gaps in biology. And honestly, in that meeting, I learned that if I went to URI, I could tackle those gaps, and work with one of the literal queens of deep-sea research. I felt hopeful and excited. I thought, “This is a good sign.”

But the next few months brought a lot of not-so-great news.

In the same week I was accepted to URI, I interviewed with Boston University, with a professor doing deep-sea microbial research. Since I was already at BU, I’d heard a lot about him, young, high energy, and his lab had literally just published a paper that went VIRAL about oxygen being produced in the deep sea without light... What??? Needless to say, I was nervous. He even personally emailed applicants and mentioned that my application was one of the top ones. You’d think that would calm my nerves, but it somehow made me even more anxious. I asked my lab mates for help, and they reassured me that he wasn’t going to grill me with questions, and they were right. But that didn’t stop me from overthinking everything. Looking back, I remember forcing my answers in the interview, not having that natural back-and-forth I had with other mentors. Long story short, Boston University rejected me on February 2, 2025. I can’t help but laugh, I literally worked there and still got denied. As an islander would say, “No family discount.” Haha. I later got feedback that I wasn’t selected because I didn’t fit the microfluidics and geobiological focus of the project. Yeah... I still don’t know how to feel about that. Haha.

Getting rejected by BU really did a number on me. I remember feeling so embarrassed to tell my BU mentor that I got rejected by the very school, and program, he works closely with. Thankfully, my BU mentor is one of the best mentors in the world and didn’t make me feel bad about it. Haha.

At this point, I was holding out hope for good news from Yale. Even though I hadn’t been invited to their Prospective Student Day, I was still optimistic. My very, very logical reasoning? The universe can’t possibly give me two rejections in the same month. Well... on February 9, 2025, I got rejected by Yale. Welp.

Early February was brutal for my mental health. On top of rejections, I got an unexpected update from UH Manoa, the mentor I applied to work with wasn’t actually part of the Microbiology program. That meant I’d either have to find a sponsor within Microbiology, convince my mentor to become a faculty member in that department, or switch to the Oceanography program. Fortunately, the graduate chair who reached out said I was a strong candidate and offered to help me navigate the situation.

During this chaotic time, I met with the professor I applied to work with, numerous times. And every meeting just reinforced what a great mentor he was. He reminded me of a former mentor I had back in Guam, very laid-back and grounded. I loved that lab in Guam. It felt like a literal blessing that he had been reading the same papers I’d been obsessing over on dissolved organic matter. Our research interests definitely aligned.

As I dug deeper into his work, I realized he’s a big name in the world of marine microbes. But what really stuck with me was one conversation where he acknowledged the importance of my goal to bring back what I learn during my PhD to Guam. That meant a lot. It was in that moment I realized UH Manoa wasn’t just a backup, it was quickly becoming a top choice.

Then on April 9, 2025, everything changed when the fire nation.... I mean when I found out I was one of the recipients of the NSF GRFP. Now I had a real decision to make: Hawaii or URI?

Ultimately, I chose Hawaii. As my BU mentor said, “Hawaii is a no-brainer.” And honestly, he was right. 

There were four big reasons:

I would have an amazing mentor, research I care deeply about, a strong community in both academia and outside it, and most importantly: I am familliar with the island culture.

What about Oregon State University?
Oh yeah... I got rejected around April 11, 2025.

Long story short, after I informed them that I was an NSF recipient, an option opened up to join as a PhD student around the 15th. As tempting as it was to start the process, I never did. Because again, UH Manoa was the no-brainer.
